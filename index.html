Snowjelly

Real-Time Snow and Jelly Simulation with MLS-MPM

Summary

Snowjelly is a real-time, interactive 3D simulator for snow and jelly that runs in the browser on WebGPU. One Moving Least Squares Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) solver drives both materials; only the constitutive model changes, so snow fractures and packs while jelly squashes and bounces back. Users throw snowballs, drop jelly blocks, and switch materials live.

Problem Description

The problem. Materials such as snow sit between solids and fluids: they hold a shape, then fracture, compact, and pile up. Mesh-based solids handle fracture poorly, and particle fluid solvers cannot hold a shape. The Material Point Method (MPM) handles both by carrying material state on particles and solving momentum on a background grid. Most MPM results are offline renders that take minutes per frame. We want the same behavior at interactive rates, in a browser tab, with no install.

Why it matters. Real-time simulation of deformable and granular materials is what games, VR, and interactive tools need. It also shows a central idea of physically based animation: one solver produces very different materials when only the stress model changes. A side-by-side of snow and jelly makes that idea visible.

Where it is challenging.

  • Parallel particle-to-grid transfer. Many particles write to the same grid node at once. A GPU compute shader needs atomic operations or a scatter-free reformulation to avoid data races, and WGSL atomics work only on integers, so values must be encoded in fixed point.
  • Snow plasticity on the GPU. The snow model clamps the singular values of each particle's elastic deformation gradient every step. That requires a robust 3x3 SVD written in WGSL, in single precision.
  • Stability. Explicit MPM is limited by a small time step. Stiff snow parameters can blow up, so we must balance stiffness, substeps per frame, and frame rate.
  • Reading the result. Tens of thousands of particles look like noise unless they are shaded and shadowed well enough to show volume, cracks, and compaction.

General approach.

  1. Build a 2D CPU reference of MLS-MPM in TypeScript to validate the math and to debug against.
  2. Port to 3D WebGPU compute shaders: particle-to-grid, grid update with boundary and collider handling, grid-to-particle. Use APIC transfers and quadratic B-spline weights.
  3. Jelly: fixed corotated hyperelastic model.
  4. Snow: the elastoplastic model of Stomakhin et al. 2013. Add singular-value clamping (critical compression and stretch) and hardening to the jelly model.
  5. Render particles as shaded sphere impostors with a shadow map. Add camera controls, a parameter panel, and tools to launch snowballs and drop shapes.

Goals and Deliverables

Baseline plan (what we will deliver)

  • A 3D MLS-MPM solver running entirely in WebGPU compute shaders.
  • Two materials from the same solver: jelly (elastic) and snow (elastoplastic with hardening).
  • At least 20,000 particles at 30 FPS or better on a laptop GPU.
  • Collisions with the domain walls, the ground, and at least one movable collider (a sphere or box the user drags).
  • Interaction: orbit camera, launch a snowball, drop a jelly shape, reset the scene, and adjust key parameters (stiffness, hardening, gravity, particle count).
  • Rendering: shaded sphere impostors with a directional light and shadow map.
  • Three demo scenes: a jelly block dropping and bouncing; a snowball smashing into a wall; the same shape dropped as jelly and as snow, side by side.
  • A public web page hosting the live demo, the report, and the video.

Aspirational plan (if we are ahead of schedule)

  • A third material: water (weakly compressible fluid) or sand (Drucker-Prager plasticity).
  • Multi-material scenes, for example a jelly block falling into a snow pile.
  • Better snow shading: screen-space ambient occlusion and a soft subsurface look.
  • Scale to 100,000 particles in real time.
  • Mesh-to-particle sampling, so any OBJ model can be turned into snow or jelly.

How we will measure results

QuestionMeasurement
Is it real-time?FPS and milliseconds per substep at 10k, 20k, 50k, and 100k particles, on an integrated GPU and a discrete GPU
Does the GPU port pay off?Speedup over our CPU reference at the same particle count
Is the solver correct?2D GPU output compared against the CPU reference; total mass and momentum conserved through the transfers in a closed box without gravity
Is jelly elastic?A dropped block returns to its rest shape; report the residual shape error after it settles
Is snow plastic?A dropped block keeps its deformed, compacted shape; a snowball fractures on impact. Compare visually with figures from Stomakhin et al. 2013
Is it stable?Largest stable time step for each material at our chosen stiffness

Questions we plan to answer

  • How many particles can a browser simulate in real time with MPM, and which stage is the bottleneck?
  • Which snow parameters (critical compression, critical stretch, hardening) give the most convincing look under a real-time time step budget?

Final deliverables

  • Report web page with technical approach, results, and performance tables.
  • A 1 to 2 minute video showing the three demo scenes, with every member speaking.

  2. Hu, Fang, Ge, Qu, Zhu, Pradhana, Jiang. "A Moving Least Squares Material Point Method with Displacement Discontinuity and Two-Way Rigid Body Coupling." ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH), 2018. The MLS-MPM formulation we implement.
  3. Jiang, Schroeder, Selle, Teran, Stomakhin. "The Affine Particle-In-Cell Method." ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH), 2015. The APIC transfer scheme.
  4. Jiang, Schroeder, Teran, Stomakhin, Selle. "The Material Point Method for Simulating Continuous Media Phenomena." SIGGRAPH Courses, 2016. Derivations and the fixed corotated model.
  5. nialltl. "Incremental MPM." A step-by-step MLS-MPM tutorial.
  6. matsuoka-601. "WebGPU-Ocean." An open-source WebGPU MLS-MPM water simulation. We consult it for the atomics-based particle-to-grid idea only; we do not start from its code, and it has no snow or elastic materials.
  7. Klar, Gast, Pradhana, Fu, Schroeder, Jiang, Teran. "Drucker-Prager Elastoplasticity for Sand Animation." ACM Transactions on Graphics (SIGGRAPH), 2016. For the aspirational sand material.
